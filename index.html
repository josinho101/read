Liquid Rocket Propulsion Overview

Liquid rocket engine design relies on converging-diverging (de Laval) nozzles to accelerate thermal combustion gases to supersonic velocities. The design steps solved here are standard in introductory aerospace textbooks such as Rocket Propulsion Elements by George P. Sutton and Oscar Biblarz.

Reference Physical Constants

The calculator uses the following critical physical constants:

  • Universal Gas Constant (\(R_u\)): \(8314.46 \text{ J/(kmol}\cdot\text{K)}\). The specific gas constant \(R_g\) is computed as \(R_u / M\), where \(M\) is the gas molecular weight.
  • Standard Gravity (\(g_0\)): \(9.80665 \text{ m/s}^2\). Used to convert exhaust velocity to specific impulse (\(I_{sp} = C / g_0\)).
  • Atmospheric Scale Height: \(8400 \text{ m}\) (used to calculate average decay ratios at extreme altitudes).

Nozzle Flow Geometries

Conical Nozzles: Easy to fabricate, they use a straight divergent section with a standard half-angle (typically \(15^\circ\)). The divergence loss is characterized by a correction factor \(\lambda = \frac{1 + \cos\theta_{div}}{2} \approx 0.983\) for \(15^\circ\).

Bell Nozzles: Designed to minimize divergence losses and nozzle length. A parabolic bell nozzle starts with a high expansion angle at the throat (\(\theta_n \approx 20^\circ \text{ to } 35^\circ\)) to expand the gases quickly, and exits at a shallower angle (\(\theta_e \approx 5^\circ \text{ to } 15^\circ\)) to align the exit flow with the engine axis. The length is typically compared to a \(15^\circ\) cone; an "80% bell" is 20% shorter than its conical equivalent but matches or exceeds its performance.
